# What the prompts are built on

Legacy's guided prompts (`prompts.js`) are based on palliative-care and
bereavement research, not guesswork. This is a summary of that research
(November 2026).

**Caveat:** most findings come from abstracts and secondary summaries, because
full texts weren't reachable. Check any exact figure against the primary paper
before quoting it publicly.

## What families wish they'd had

- **Unfinished business makes grief harder.** Among 224 bereaved adults, having
  unfinished business, and being distressed by it, went with poorer bereavement
  outcomes. It clusters in conflicted or anxious relationships.
  ([Klingspon et al., *Death Studies* 2015](https://synapse.mskcc.org/synapse/works/76720);
  [UBBS](https://synapse.mskcc.org/synapse/works/157734))
- A Japanese scale for families of cancer patients splits unfinished business
  into **things left unsaid, things left undone, and messages never given**.
  ([Matsuzaka et al. 2025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12410324/))
- Adults who lost a parent before age 12 most wished for **specific
  remembrances and communication, the parent's values and feelings about them,
  and personal objects**.
  ([Muriel et al., *Death Studies* 2019](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/30623743))
- What a final conversation is **about** (identity, love, practical matters,
  everyday life) matters more than whether a "last talk" happened at all.
  ([Scheinfeld et al. 2024](https://comm.uky.edu/kchc/program/schedule/2024/paperposter/2165))

**What this means for Legacy:** a "Someone specific" path that names the
person; prompts about how you feel about *them*; an "Everyday me" category for
the small details people miss; and a "Gratitude & repair" category for things
left unsaid.

## Dignity Therapy (Chochinov)

Dignity Therapy is a structured interview that becomes a written document for
family. Its questions include:
- Tell me about your life history: what you remember most, and when you felt
  most alive.
- Is there anything you want your family to know or remember about you?
- What are the most important roles you've played?
- What are your hopes and dreams for your loved ones?
- What have you learned that you'd want to pass on?
- Is there anything else you'd like included?

The evidence:
- In the 2011 *Lancet Oncology* randomized trial, patients rated it more
  helpful than the comparison arms and said it changed how their family saw
  them. It did **not** significantly reduce distress, which was the trial's
  primary outcome.
- **78% of family members said the document helped them in their grief, and
  95% would recommend it.**
  ([McClement et al. 2007](https://read.qxmd.com/read/17985964/dignity-therapy-family-member-perspectives))
- Therapists are trained to **edit out content that could hurt a recipient**,
  with the patient's agreement.

## Other frameworks

- **Byock's Four Things:** "Please forgive me." "I forgive you." "Thank you."
  "I love you." These are the basis of the "Gratitude & repair" category.
  ([overview](https://www.fivewishes.org/blogs/recommendation/the-four-things-that-matter-most-ira-byock-md))
- **Ethical wills** pass on values, beliefs and lessons rather than property.
  Their tone tends to be nostalgic and forward-giving.
  ([scoping review](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8680808/))
  These are the basis of "What I believe" and "Advice & lessons".

## Emotional safety

**Bitterness revival**, meaning rehashing old grievances, is linked to
depression and anxiety. Life review appears to help partly by steering away
from it.
([Westerhof, Bohlmeijer & Webster 2010](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/ageing-and-society/article/reminiscence-and-mental-health-a-review-of-recent-progress-in-theory-research-and-interventions/A92B78BECF29F386309138DCBED28531))

How Legacy applies this:

| Principle | In the app |
|---|---|
| Every prompt is optional | "Skip and just start" on the guide; **Skip** on every follow-up; **Not this one** on family questions; **Not now** on photo moments. No streaks, no nagging. |
| Light topics first | Category order puts *Someone specific* and *Everyday me* before *Gratitude & repair*, which shows a gentle pacing note. |
| Never prompt for trauma | No "worst moment" questions anywhere in the library. |
| Don't over-direct the recipient | Prompts lean on "what would you tell…" and "what do you hope…", not "what should they…". |
| Review before release | Everything defaults to **Just me**. Messages to someone default to **Family**, but nothing leaves the device until you export a family edition. |

## Not yet built, but the research points to it

- **Age-gated message series** for children: one letter can't speak to a
  6-year-old and a 30-year-old.
- A gentle **"will this be kind to them?"** check before a message is handed
  on, following Dignity Therapy's editing step.
- **Cooling-off and delivery controls** for sensitive disclosures and
  apologies to estranged people.
- A nudge to **include every sibling** when writing to one.

## What couldn't be verified

- The exact wording of several Dignity Therapy questions.
- The sample sizes in the 2011 trial.
- Prevalence figures for unfinished business.
- **Any peer-reviewed study of how recipients experience future-dated
  milestone letters.** The cautions above come from practice guidance and
  grief theory, not trials.
